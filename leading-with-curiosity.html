A Framework of My Own

Leading with Curiosity

Not a model I borrowed — the operating system I have actually run for forty years, from a sales team in 1985 to a 130,000-person company. Born curious. The rest is a choice.

The needle is set to explore. The ring turning inside it is the first three points at work:

  • State a prior
  • Ask for the evidence
  • Update prior

Drill One

The six points, one by one

1

Start at “I don’t know.”

Say it first, say it in public, then find the real experts and say “teach me” — and mean it.

The story

In 1985, at twenty-three, I was put in charge of salespeople aged thirty-five to fifty-eight, including a 58-year-old legend, the “God of Sales”. On day one I told him: “I don’t know anything about sales. I’d love you to teach me.” As Genpact’s CEO I was still saying “I don’t understand. Please explain it to me” in rooms of twenty — until the whole company learned it was safe to say “I don’t know”.

The practiceOpen all meetings with this admission, not the answer.

2

Ask, don’t tell.

Answers are now free; the question is the work, and the scarcest skill in the room.

The story

My rule, restated across forty years: “stop answering and start asking”. Asking profound questions is the human edge.

The practiceMeasure a meeting by the quality of its questions, not its answers.

3

Carry a prior, not a position.

Every assumption is a prior: state it, hunt the evidence that tests it, and above all, ask people. Update it in public.

The story

My discipline, in three moves: state it out loud, invite people to attack it, change it in public — then go back and tell whoever changed my mind: “I changed my assumption — because of you.” That is how permission to challenge cascades down a company.

The practiceThe posterior of this meeting is the prior of the next.

4

Hire the hunger. Build the university.

Recruit for hunger and ability to learn over knowledge; make the environment feed both.

The story

Our founding bet at Genpact — “Hire People Who Know Nothing”: we hired raw material who knew nothing about the client’s business and had the client teach them. At the entrance stood a second board: “Trespassers Will Be Hired.” Hiring went from 25 to 100 to 2,000 a month; 130,000 people.

The practiceSecond chances for poor performance — never for wrong values.

5

Feed it every day.

Curiosity runs on a system, not a mood: capture everything, read and listen across fields, put ideas out for reaction.

The story

My field guide: 28 podcasts, about 100 hours a month, across business, AI, history, astronomy and science. A photographed paragraph from a book, a voice note dictated mid-podcast, a museum plaque translated on the spot — “Capture is what turns curiosity into a body of knowledge.”

The practicePost to learn, not to broadcast.

6

Keep the dial on explore.

Life is the balance between exploring and exploiting; in the age of AI, the exploring is the human job — flanked by trust and judgment.

The story

Explore gave humans fire, the wheel, agriculture. Two hundred thousand years of civilisation, built by people stepping into the unknown. Exploit only, without explore, and one day you run out of new information.

The practiceIn the first hundred days of anything: explore first.

Drill Two

What I believe

Why a spiral, not a circle: you never return to the same belief.

The third point of this framework, written as an essay, is Every Model Begins with a Prior — the story of Bayes’s theorem.

It’s good to have assumptions so that you can constantly test them. It’s bad to have assumptions that you don’t test.

I changed my assumption — because of you.

Curiosity is the single most important quality I hire and promote for.

Stop answering and start asking.

Capture is what turns curiosity into a body of knowledge.

Other people may hold the knowns for your unknown unknowns.

Born curious. The rest is a choice.

The Essay

The full story

In 1985 I was twenty-three years old, fresh out of business school, and put in charge of a sales team of seven people aged thirty-five to fifty-eight. One of them was a legend — the God of Sales, a man who had forgotten more about selling than I would ever know. On my first day I said the only true thing I could say to him: “I don’t know anything about sales. I’d love you to teach me.”

I have been opening rooms that way ever since.

Forty years later, running a company of 130,000 people, I was still saying it — in rooms of twenty, in front of my own leadership team: “I don’t understand. Please explain it to me.” Until the whole company learned it was safe to say “I don’t know.” That is where curiosity actually starts. Not with a technique. With an admission.

It matters more now than it did in 1985, because the economics have flipped. Answers are getting cheaper every quarter — AI has seen to that. When everyone has the answers, power shifts to those who ask the questions. Asking profound questions is the human edge, and it is a skill most leaders have quietly let rust, because for decades the room rewarded the person who answered fastest.

But a question is only half the discipline. The other half is what you do with your beliefs while you wait for better evidence. I carry every assumption as a prior. It is good to have assumptions — so that you can constantly test them. It is bad to have assumptions you don’t test. So I state mine out loud, I invite people to attack them, and when the evidence moves, I update, in public. Then I go back to the person who changed my mind and tell them: “I changed my assumption — because of you.” That is how permission to challenge cascades down a company. It is also, not coincidentally, how Bayes’ theorem works — every judgment is a prior meeting evidence and coming out a posterior. The posterior of one meeting is simply the prior of the next. Keep the wheel turning and you never stop learning; let it stop and you are a know-it-all with a title.

At Genpact we built the whole company on the hiring version of this idea. Our founding bet was to hire people who knew nothing about the client’s business and have the client teach them — because knowing something is not important; you can learn. What matters is a hunger to learn, an ability to learn, and an environment that feeds both. We put a second board at the entrance next to the one listing our values: “Trespassers Will Be Hired.” We built a company that feels like a university, where learning is the job, at every scale, forever. Curiosity scales. Résumés don’t.

None of this runs on mood. It runs on a system. I capture everything — a photographed paragraph from a book, a voice note dictated mid-podcast, papers pulled and simplified, museum plaques translated on the spot — because in the moment everything feels memorable, and almost none of it is. Capture is what turns curiosity into a body of knowledge. I listen to twenty-eight podcasts, a hundred hours a month, across business, science, history and the night sky. I put ideas out in public to learn, not to broadcast — other people hold the knowns for my unknown unknowns, and their reactions confirm, negate and reshape what I think.

Underneath it all is one setting on one dial. Every person and every company lives somewhere between exploring and exploiting — between stepping into the unknown and milking what is already known. Exploring built human civilisation. It began with fire and the wheel. Exploiting matters — explore only, and you starve. But exploit only, and one day you run out of new information. In the age of AI the exploiting is increasingly the machine’s job. The exploring remains the human home ground.

We are all born curious. We peak in curiosity at the age of four, and then life trains it out of us — school, compliance, the fear of looking stupid in the meeting. I have spent forty years refusing that training in myself.

Born curious. The rest is a choice.
