I'm Tiger Tyagarajan — CEO of Genpact (2011–2024), now a board member and senior advisor on AI, transformation and how organisations learn. My talks, frameworks, writing and enthusiasms, in one place.

What I stand for

My name is my operating manual. Tyāga — त्याग — is the Bhagavad Gita's word for renunciation, precisely defined: do the work with everything you have, and give up attachment to the fruits. Tyagarajan means “king of renunciation”. I can't claim the serenity — but I recognise the discipline, and its unlikely twin — curiosity.

Sixty years of being curious — an insatiable, slightly desperate need to keep learning, and therefore to keep asking questions — pulling me to the edge of change in industry after industry, and never letting me settle for what I already knew.

This site is where the two meet.

Giving back — knowledge, experience, scars, half-formed views — without attachment to how they land. And holding every view the Bayesian way, the way human beings have actually made progress for 3,000 years: state your prior, meet the evidence, update.

In a world where the unknown unknowns beat everything else we think we know, that isn't a philosophy. It's survival.

So read. Argue. Correct me. The continuous updating is the point. And that only happens by being curious, asking questions, debating, and changing.

FEATURED ESSAY

Born Curious, Taught to Comply

An essay by Tiger Tyagarajan

We peak in curiosity at the age of four.

Sit with that for a moment. The most relentless learning machines any of us will ever be are the small people asking why for the fourteenth time in an hour — and everything that follows, the schooling, the socialising, the long professional training in having answers, is in large part a managed decline from that peak.

FEATURED ESSAY

The Answers Got Cheap

An essay by Tiger Tyagarajan

Something happened in October 2026 that I cannot stop thinking about.

In a single month, a frontier AI model produced serious mathematical results across seventeen fields of mathematics — 719 manuscripts, hundreds of result families. Problems the community had circled for decades, some for a century, suddenly had machine-generated proofs attached to them: a blowup construction for the Navier–Stokes equations, the quasi-Riemann hypothesis pushed past barriers that had held for generations, a resolution of the Unique Games Conjecture.
