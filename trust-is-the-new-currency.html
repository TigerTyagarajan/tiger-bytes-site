Trust Is the New Currency

In July 2026 I went back into Adam Pacifico’s Leadership Enigma studio — this time with Angela Cretu, who ran Avon as its global CEO and, like me, now leads a plural life. Adam had spent six years and more than 300 conversations asking one question: as machines become more intelligent, what makes leadership more human? His answer is a framework of eight dimensions — vulnerability, inclusivity, humility, empathy, personal purpose, presence, care and curiosity — and that day he launched the first Global Human-Centered Leadership Index to measure them. Angela and I were there to argue with it. Here is what I took away.

AI flips the ratio. We spend perhaps 5% of our time asking questions and 95% finding answers, building solutions, fixing mistakes. The machine compresses the 95%. What remains — what expands to fill the time — is the asking. And if everyone has the same machine and gets the same answer, the only differentiator left is what is human.

You can have the answer and still change it. Angela put it sharply: the machine gives everyone the same solution; the leader’s job is sometimes to say, I know — and I’m changing it, because my values and my purpose point somewhere else. Only a human can change the answer.

Stability in the foundation. Instability everywhere else. Don’t promise your team a stable world; it doesn’t exist. Make a thin layer — purpose, meaning, values — absolutely non-negotiable, and keep everything else deliberately unstable. Teams with all their KPIs green make me nervous: I used to move their two best people out. Instability forces new thinking; progress is made in it, not despite it.

Curiosity is a loop, and trust runs underneath it. You become curious by being humble — saying “I don’t know” to the person on the front line and asking them to teach you — by being vulnerable about your fears and failures, and by including the people who disagree with you. That loop is how learning happens. And underneath all eight dimensions sits trust: in a world of machine output you cannot verify, you believe an answer because of who sent it. Trust becomes the currency.

Presence is two disciplines. Angela’s is immersion: three years at a time in other people’s cultures — she told us about her first town hall in Russia, after which she was informed she had smiled too much (“In Russia we have a saying: smile and everyone will think you are an idiot”). Mine is attention: the phone face-down at dinner for two hours, and being in the moment — the past only for blameless learning loops (our two-hour post-mortems named no names), the future only for scenarios.

Empathy is the gap. Both of us, unprompted, named the same missing dimension. Leaders all know the word; too few practise it. An AI change journey is 10% technology, 20% process and 70% people — and yet thousands of people still learn they’ve lost their jobs by email. Angela’s question hangs over every automation roadmap: who will buy the products of the beautifully automated company?

Would they vote for you? Angela asks herself, almost daily: if my people could vote at 9 a.m. today, would they choose me? “They don’t choose you for how colourful you are. They choose you for the colour you bring to their lives.” And the scoreboard is not your tenure. It is five years later — whether what you built is doing better without you, and whether people still say they were part of something. People follow a seat automatically; the leaders worth becoming attract followers with no seat at all.

Adam is now taking the Index to leaders around the world — he hopes at least 500 will take part. My prediction: the averages will be less interesting than the spread — the spikes by geography, culture and industry, and the ones nobody can explain, which will throw up the next round of questions. That’s as it should be. In a world of artificial intelligence, the future belongs to leaders who go back to where they came from: their humanity.
